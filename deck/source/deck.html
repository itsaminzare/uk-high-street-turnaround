Fixing Northbridge's high street

A five-year plan to bring town-centre shop vacancy down from 18% to the GB average of 14% by 2030

Mohammadamin Zare · October 2026
Northbridge is an illustrative composite town; all data is real and public
Executive summary

Shopping alone will not refill Northbridge: shrink the retail core and give people other reasons to come

Northbridge should concentrate retail into a compact core, fill empty units fast using its new powers, and anchor the centre with food, leisure and homes.

Pillar 1

Right-size the centre

27.5% of retail spending is now online and isn't coming back. Struggling towns have more than twice the retail space per person of thriving ones.

Pillar 2

Fill empty units fast

High Street Rental Auctions let the council let units empty for a year without landlord consent. Independents, 84% of closures, face a 28% rates rise in 2026/27.

Pillar 3

Give people reasons to come

In thriving centres food is £1 in every £4 spent, against £1 in £10 in struggling ones. A food and leisure anchor, events and town-centre homes bring footfall back.

Sources: ONS; Centre for Retail Research; BRC–Local Data Company; Centre for Cities; England business rates 2024–2027. Full list on the last slide.2
The question

We turned "what do we do about the high street?" into one answerable question

How can Northbridge Council bring town-centre shop vacancy down from about 18% to 14% by 2030, while increasing footfall, using its existing powers and about £5m of capital?
✓ Specific✓ Measurable✓ Action-oriented✓ Relevant✓ Time-bound

Decision-maker

Council leader and cabinet, who need a fundable plan with visible results within 12 months.

In scope

  • Town-centre units
  • Planning, rental auctions, grants
  • Council property, events, parking

Out of scope

  • National tax policy (taken as given)
  • Out-of-town retail parks
  • Online retail itself
Northbridge's 18% vacancy matches the North East average (17.9%, Q4 2023, Local Data Company). GB average 14.0%.3
Approach

We tested eight hypotheses across four MECE branches; two were wrong, and that changed the answer

✕ RefutedThe 2026 rates reform lowers small shops' costs

Each branch is one reason a unit stays empty: too few customers, wrong space, too costly, or nobody filling it. Every unit falls into exactly one.4
Findings · A. Demand

More than a quarter of retail spending is now online, and most of the pandemic jump has stuck

72%of the pandemic jump in online share has stuck: 19.2% in 2019, 30.7% in 2021, 27.5% in 2025
  • Footfall is still drifting down: −0.8% across 2025, −2.9% in December
  • The spending that left is not coming back to physical shops
So whatNorthbridge needs reasons to visit that the internet can't replace.
Source: ONS Retail Sales Index, series J4MC; BRC–Sensormatic footfall monitor (9 Jan 2026).5
Findings · B. Supply

Retail parks recovered after the pandemic; high streets did not, and struggling towns simply have too much space

  • Retail park vacancy fell from 11.3% to 7.6%; high streets barely moved (14.5% → 14.0%)
  • Newport, Wigan and Middlesbrough have more than twice the retail space per person of Brighton or Liverpool
  • North East vacancy (17.9%) is almost 70% higher than London's (10.6%)
So whatEven a successful plan won't fill every unit as a shop. Shrink the retail footprint and repurpose the rest.
Source: BRC–Local Data Company Vacancy Monitor (Q3 2021–Q4 2023); Centre for Cities, Checking out (July 2025).6
Findings · C. Cost

The "permanent cut" in business rates still raises a small shop's bill by 28% in 2026/27

  • From April 2026, a new lower multiplier (38.2p) replaces the 40% relief retail had in 2025/26
  • For a shop with a rateable value of £30,000, the bill rises from £8,982 to £11,460, three times its 2024/25 level
  • This hypothesis was refuted: rates are not a problem already solved by government
So whatA one-year rates transition grant keeps viable independents open through the cliff.
Source: Armstrong Watson (May 2025); Spelthorne Borough Council 2026/27 multipliers. Illustrative: ignores the 2026 revaluation and transitional relief.7
Findings · C. Cost

Independents, not chains, now account for 5 in 6 store closures

84%of 2024's 13,479 closures were independents (11,341)
  • Chain closures peaked in 2019, 2020 and 2022, and have fallen since
  • About 17,350 closures were forecast for 2025, up 29% on 2024
So whatKeeping the independents already there matters as much as attracting national chains.
Source: Centre for Retail Research (2018–2024 actual; 2025 forecast).8
Recommendations

Three pillars: right-size the centre, fill empty units fast, and give people reasons to come

Pillar 1

Right-size the centre

  • Define a compact retail core of 2–3 streets
  • Rezone the edges for homes, health, education and workspace
  • Start conversions with council-owned property
Because: too much retail space for the spending left (findings A, B)
Pillar 2

Fill empty units fast

  • High Street Rental Auctions on units empty more than a year
  • Meanwhile-use and pop-up programme
  • 2026/27 rates transition grant for small independents
Because: new powers exist, and independents face a rates cliff (findings C, D)
Pillar 3

Give people reasons to come

  • A food and leisure anchor, such as a market hall in a large empty unit
  • A year-round events programme
  • Prioritise new homes in the town centre
Because: strong centres win on food, leisure and residents (findings A, D)
Recommendations draw on Centre for Cities (2025) and the High Street Rental Auction regulations (in force 2 December 2024).9

Risks and next steps

The main risks are landlord resistance and capital; both have clear mitigations

RiskMitigation
Landlords resist auctions or conversionsStart with the longest-empty units, publish the data, offer partnership before compulsory powers
£5m is too little for conversionsUse council property first; bid for national regeneration funding; joint ventures with housing associations
Pop-ups don't become lasting tenantsTrack conversion to longer leases; give successful pop-ups priority in auctions
National policy changes againReview the rates grant yearly; build the plan on demand and supply, not tax

Next steps

  • Cabinet: agree the core boundary and auction area
  • Month 1–3: unit-by-unit vacancy survey to set baselines
  • Within 6 months: first rental auction, to show early results
